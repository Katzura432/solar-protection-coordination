Protection coordination with distributed solar — engineering report

Generated by the executed MATLAB study. MATLAB 25.2.0.2998904 (R2025b); seed 20261004.

1. Objective and network

Design and test selective feeder protection with current-limited solar generation. The 11 kV, 60 Hz radial feeder has four protected sections, 5 MW + 1.25 Mvar of nominal fixed-impedance load, and 0–4 MW of PV at bus 3. Solar penetration is installed PV divided by the 5 MW nominal active load: 0–80%.

Source positive/negative-sequence impedance is 0.12 + j1.20 ohm; zero sequence is 0.25 + j1.80 ohm. Source impedance scales from 0.8 to 1.5 in the design envelope. Line zero-sequence impedance is three times positive sequence. The source is grounded, loads are grounded wye, and inverter injection has positive sequence only. See src/study_config.m for every network parameter.

2. Electrical and inverter models

This is a three-phase RMS/phasor protection study, not an electromagnetic-transient or switching-inverter model. A complex 15-node phase-domain nodal admittance solve computes voltages, CT-location line currents, and fault currents. Each faulted line is split at its specified fault location. Breaker opening removes its sending-end connection; the downstream section and inverter remain electrically represented.

The inverter uses an ideal source-angle reference and voltage-dependent active/reactive current commands. Reactive current receives priority during voltage sag; total phase current is limited to 1.20 times installed rated current. Available active output is installed rating times irradiance. The solved current command depends on actual terminal voltage. There is no detailed DC link, PLL, negative-sequence control, switching ripple, or vendor-specific fault response. This ideal synchronized current-source abstraction can continue energizing an island until its intertie opens; direct transfer trip is explicitly modeled rather than assuming instant solar disconnection.

3. Protection settings and coordination

The relay has independent operate accumulators for phases A/B/C and residual current 3I0. The IEC normal-inverse equation is t = TMS * 0.14 / ((I/Ipickup)^0.02 - 1) above pickup. Each enabled channel integrates dt/t; dropout resets its accumulator and a trip latches. Current is specified in primary RMS amps; CT ratios and equivalent 1 A-secondary settings are tabulated below.

Conventional 51/51N uses no direction supervision. The directional scheme adds phase-by-phase memory-voltage polarization with a 60 degree characteristic angle and zero-sequence voltage polarization for ground direction. Phase memory uses the ideal nominal source reference; this abstraction is not a full commercial relay polarization algorithm. The improved scheme additionally restrains R1 phase pickup using max(0.5, min(1,Vmin_pu)^2) and coordinates TMS across the full DER envelope. R1 voltage restraint addresses weak source-current backup sensitivity found at high solar penetration.

Forward load-envelope phase pickup has 20% reserve and is rounded upward in 5 A steps. Residual pickup has 50% reserve with explicit minimum settings. Each relay shares one TMS across its four channels. Fixed pickups and a radial relay hierarchy make minimum-TMS grading transparent: solve R2 after R3, then R1 after R2/R4. Each bound is TMS_up >= (K_down*TMS_down + 0.25 + 0.05 + 0.001) / K_up, where K is the minimum enabled unit-TMS channel time. TMS is rounded upward in 0.005 steps. This is constrained grading of fixed pickups, not a global optimization of all possible relay designs.

The 0.25 s required coordination margin is measured from primary breaker opening to the prospective backup relay command, with 0.05 s breaker time and a 1 ms allowance. R1 backs up R2/R4; R2 backs up R3; R1 provides another level of backup for R3. Opening B1/B2/B3 sends a direct PV transfer trip with 20 ms communication delay and 40 ms PV-intertie breaker time. B4 opening leaves PV grid-connected. No automatic reclosing is modeled.

4. Test design

The design envelope contains 7200 cases: four faulted sections, ten fault types, locations 0.1/0.5/0.9 of each section, fault resistances 0.1/2/10 ohm, PV ratings 0/1/2/3/4 MW, two source-impedance scales, and two load scales (0.25 and 1.2). Load unbalance is 3%. ABC denotes a balanced three-phase-to-ground fault; ABG/BCG/CAG are explicit interphase-plus-ground conductance networks.

Independent validation uses 180 newly randomized scenarios with continuously varying fault location, resistance, PV rating, irradiance, source impedance, load scale, and unbalance. These scenarios are not used to set TMS. Dynamic evaluation includes 360 randomly selected design cases, the independent validation cases, no-fault operating profiles, primary-breaker failure cases, and three intentionally disabled-transfer-trip cases. Every scenario runs all three protection schemes.

Event-driven simulation exactly integrates stationary RMS channel rates between circuit-changing events. The live Simulink model separately runs at 1 ms and feeds breaker states back into the circuit with a unit delay. Neither simulation includes a waveform-derived phasor measurement delay. Results are simulation results, not field or hardware validation.

6. Verification and practical limits

Independent Thevenin and sequence-network formulas verify balanced and AG fault currents with no loads/PV. All design cases meet nodal KCL residual and inverter current-limit checks. Improved design cases meet 0.25 s grading. Dynamic tests verify no-fault behavior, fault isolation, primary-breaker backup, sustained inverter contribution when transfer trip is disabled, and a two-breaker-failure source-backup case. Every live Simulink measurement is checked against a separate circuit solve; relay/breaker timings agree with the event-driven reference within two 1 ms steps. Exact checks are in results/verification.txt.

The maximum primary trip delay in the improved design envelope is 10.728 s, compared with 17.151 s for the conventional settings. High-resistance, near-pickup faults can therefore remain slow despite meeting grading and sensitivity requirements. This project imposes no universal maximum-clearing-time requirement. A practical study would need equipment damage curves, acceptable fault-duration limits, more sensitive elements or communication-assisted protection where required, and manufacturer-specific settings validation.

Further limits: ideal CTs/VTs, no CT saturation, no harmonics, no arc model, fixed-impedance loads, no transformer energization, no finite relay memory decay, no neutral conductor model, and a simplified inverter. The fault-resistance range is not a validation of high-impedance arcing-fault detection. Meeting this study envelope does not certify compliance with an IEC or IEEE standard.

The severe two-breaker-failure sensitivity check requires about 31 s for source backup. This is an intentional diagnostic of near-pickup inverse-time delay, not a claim of acceptable utility protection speed. Communication-assisted or differential protection would need investigation where equipment fault-duration limits rule out such delayed backup. Six additional saved-model scenarios verify breaker failure, conventional sympathetic trips, directional selectivity, no-fault reverse export, source-zone isolation and disabled transfer trip.
